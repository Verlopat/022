# Introduction

Take one camera frame from the west arm of a Bengaluru junction during the evening peak. Two-wheelers stand three abreast at the stop line, auto-rickshaws fill the gaps between them, a bus straddles both lanes, and the queue runs past the top edge of the frame. A signal controller that reads this frame has to answer three questions within a few seconds. How much demand does the arm hold, including the part the camera cannot see? How much more will arrive before the arm gets green again? Can the downstream link absorb the vehicles that a long green would release? A fixed-time plan answers none of these questions, and a controller that reads only its own arms answers the first one poorly and ignores the other two.

Indian urban junctions make these questions harder than the settings in which most adaptive controllers were developed. Many junctions run split phasing, in which each arm receives its own phase, because right-turning volumes are heavy and lane discipline is weak. With four phases and 5 s of intergreen per phase, 20 s of every cycle carry no traffic, so every second of green given to an arm that has already emptied is withheld from three arms that are still queued. Traffic is also heterogeneous. Two-wheelers make up about half of the flow, three-wheelers and cars most of the rest, and a single bus can occupy the discharge time of five or six two-wheelers. A raw vehicle count misstates demand, and passenger car units (PCU) are the working currency of Indian capacity analysis.

Current practice and research offer partial answers. Fixed-time plans follow Webster's delay model, which gives the delay-minimising cycle and flow-ratio splits for known average demand. SCATS adapts cycle length and splits from stop-line detectors and ends phases early when demand stops, but inductive loops assume lane discipline and uniform vehicle lengths, and they are costly to maintain in cities that resurface roads often. Deep reinforcement learning controllers, including graph attention designs such as CoLight and attention models such as AttendLight, have reduced delay in simulation, yet they learn black-box policies over long training runs in stationary simulated worlds and read lane states that a real detector does not deliver. On the sensing side, single-stage detectors have made camera counting practical, and the UVH-26 dataset from about 2,800 Bengaluru Safe-City cameras now supplies 14 India-specific vehicle classes on which trained detectors outperform COCO-trained ones by a wide margin. The cameras already exist; the missing piece is a controller that turns their frames into coordinated green time.

The simplest camera controller, used in many density-based systems, scores each arm from a snapshot and splits a fixed cycle in proportion to the scores. The starting point of this work was a desktop prototype of this kind: it ran a UVH-26-trained YOLO model on a CPU, sampled one image per arm per cycle at three junctions, split a user-set cycle in proportion to class-weighted scores and displayed GAT-based junction impact scores that did not feed back into the timing. This local proportional split (LPS) has three blind spots. First, the camera field of view ends about 60 m from the stop line while the link behind it is 250-300 m long, so the score saturates exactly when the queue is longest. Second, the score ignores vehicles that the upstream junction is about to release. Third, green given to an arm whose downstream link is full is wasted, because blocked vehicles cannot discharge. Graph neural networks can address the second and third blind spots by passing information between junctions, and a learned estimator can address the first by inferring the hidden part of a queue from the visible part, the signal history and the neighbours. What remains open is how to couple such an estimator with a timing rule that traffic engineers can audit, and how much it helps once realistic detection errors from heterogeneous traffic are included.

This paper proposes GATC, a camera-only controller for split-phased multi-junction corridors. At the start of each cycle, detections from one frame per arm are converted into PCU-weighted arm scores and assembled into a 15-node graph of arm and junction nodes. A two-layer graph attention network (GAT) predicts, for every arm, the standing queue in PCU and the PCU that will arrive in the next 120 s, and for every junction the flow ratio that serves as its impact score. A timing law then sets the cycle from Webster's formula at the predicted flow ratio, discounts arms that feed loaded downstream links, and divides green in proportion to the predicted demand over the planned cycle. A tactical layer checks the stop-line zone of the arm holding green once per second and ends the phase when the zone is empty or when vehicles stop crossing. The estimator is trained by supervised learning on logs from an exploratory behaviour policy, so no reinforcement learning loop is required and every quantity entering the timing law is a physical prediction that can be compared with field counts.

The study tests three hypotheses. H1: a GAT over arm and junction nodes predicts next-cycle PCU demand more accurately than a calibrated snapshot score and than graph-free or neighbourhood-limited models, with the largest gain on internal arms fed by upstream junctions. H2: at the same one-frame-per-cycle sensing budget, the GAT-based plan reduces average waiting time relative to the local proportional controller and to Webster fixed-time plans. H3: with the tactical camera layer added, GATC matches or improves on loop-based actuated control, camera-based max-pressure control and a deep Q-network trained on the same camera frames across off-peak, peak, oversaturated, tidal and incident scenarios, and the gain holds when the UVH-26-trained detector is replaced by COCO-trained YOLOv8 models. A fourth, diagnostic question asks how much of each gain disappears when the estimator is replaced by raw camera scores or by a graph-free estimator, in each sensing regime.

The contributions are as follows.

- A camera-only, class-aware signal controller for heterogeneous traffic that couples PCU lane scoring, a 15-node junction graph and a GAT estimator with an explicit timing law and a once-per-second tactical check.
- A supervised training protocol in which targets for standing queue, arrivals and junction flow ratio are computed from vehicle passage logs, which replaces reward engineering with measurable prediction targets.
- A mesoscopic simulator of split-phased corridors with seven vehicle groups, lane blockage and spillback, verified against Webster's delay formula before use.
- A paired evaluation over five scenarios, 20 seeds and eleven controllers, including deep Q-networks trained on the same camera frames and on the exact simulator state, with ablations of every design element, detector-profile sensitivity and non-parametric tests with Holm correction.
- Evidence on where the gains come from: graph attention gives the most accurate demand estimates and auditable attention maps, whereas the waiting-time gains in split-phased corridors come from learned queue estimation, cycle adaptation and the once-per-second check, and a graph-free estimator performs as well in closed loop.
