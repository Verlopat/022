# Literature Review and Integration

## Fixed-Time and Adaptive Signal Timing

Webster's delay model remains the analytical base of signal timing. It expresses average delay on a junction arm as a uniform term, a random-arrival term and an empirical correction, and it yields the delay-minimising cycle $C_0=(1.5L+5)/(1-Y)$, where $L$ is the lost time per cycle and $Y$ the sum of critical flow ratios [@31]. Fixed-time plans built on this model are cheap and predictable. They also freeze the assumed demand, so a plan tuned for the average peak cannot follow a tidal surge, a stalled bus or a platoon released by the neighbouring junction. SCATS answered this limitation with two control levels: a strategic level that adapts cycle length, splits and offsets from the degree of saturation measured at stop-line detectors, and a tactical level that terminates phases early when demand ceases [@32]. SCATS also groups adjacent junctions into subsystems that share a cycle. Its sensing layer, the inductive loop, assumes lane discipline and homogeneous vehicle lengths, and both assumptions fail on Indian corridors where two-wheelers filter between lanes and auto-rickshaws occupy half a lane.

Surveys of computational intelligence in urban signal control catalogue fuzzy rules, neural predictors, evolutionary search and early reinforcement learning, and they report that most controllers were tested at isolated junctions under synthetic demand [@35]. Low-cost vision variants replaced loops with image processing. One such system estimated density on each arm from Canny edge maps and allocated green time in proportion to the edge count [@33]. Edge density responds to shadows, headlights and occlusion, and the allocation remains local to one junction. A communications-oriented review of traffic management for smart cities adds the deployment view: loops, magnetometers and roadside units raise installation and maintenance cost, while camera networks and wireless links shift the burden to data processing [@34]. The common thread is that classical and early adaptive controllers compute each junction in isolation or depend on sensors that heterogeneous traffic defeats.

## Learning-Based Signal Controllers

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) reframed signal timing as sequential decision making. An early deep Q-learning controller used a stacked autoencoder to estimate action values at a single junction and reduced queue length relative to a conventional reinforcement learner [@1]. Later single-junction designs refined state and reward engineering. G-DQN grouped the detection cells of each lane with proximity weights, processed position and speed matrices in a dual-channel convolutional network, and outperformed DQN, A2C and fixed timing in SUMO [@13]. A teacher-student scheme added an importance score from a pre-trained teacher to the environment reward of a student agent and was tested in CityFlow on a 4 × 4 grid and on networks from Jinan, Hangzhou and New York [@14]. A federated PPO scheme trained agents under different demand patterns, shared only network parameters, and cut average waiting time by 27.34% on average against fixed timing at a single four-arm junction [@19]. A partial-detection study trained a deep Q-network with only connected vehicles observable and identified the penetration rates below which control quality degrades [@20].

Network-level DRL adds coordination. MARLIN-ATSC coupled neighbouring agents through joint action values and, on 59 junctions of downtown Toronto, reduced average junction delay by 27% in independent mode and 39% in integrated mode [@15]. MA2C stabilised multi-agent advantage actor-critic learning with neighbour fingerprints and a spatial discount factor, with tests on a synthetic grid and the Monaco network [@16]. A single policy-gradient agent was also trained on spatio-temporal traffic data to time several junctions [@17]. SHLight organised learning as a manager-worker hierarchy in which regional managers set flow-direction goals and junction workers pursue them; it gave the lowest queues and waiting times on real networks of 26 and 19 junctions and in two of three demand settings on a 25-junction grid [@18]. A review of this literature points to recurring weaknesses: inconsistent evaluation protocols, dependence on simulators that expose perfect lane-level state, long training in stationary environments and scarce field evidence [@2]. The state vectors in most of these studies are read directly from the simulator. They do not pass through a detector that misses auto-rickshaws or confuses a tempo-traveller with a truck.

## Graph and Attention Models of Road Networks

Graph neural networks encode road topology explicitly. Graph convolutional layers inside a reinforcement learner let a controller exploit the geometry of a six-junction network and reach policies comparable to conventional reinforcement learning about twice as fast [@9]. IG-RL treated lanes and lane connections as graph nodes, shared parameters across the graph and transferred without retraining to a Manhattan-scale network of 3,971 signalised junctions [@10]. Graph attention networks (GAT) replace fixed convolution weights with learned, masked self-attention over each neighbourhood, and multi-head attention stabilises the learned coefficients [@11]. CoLight inserted graph attention into a reinforcement learner so that each junction weighs its neighbours according to the current traffic state, and it outperformed independent learners on datasets from Jinan, Hangzhou and New York [@12]. AttendLight applied attention over lanes and phases to obtain one model that serves junctions with different numbers of roads and phases [@21]. On the forecasting side, T-GCN combined graph convolution with gated recurrent units to predict link speeds [@27].

Two contrasts matter for the present study. First, in the control literature attention lives inside a reward-driven policy, so the attention weights shape actions but are never checked against a measurable physical quantity. Second, in the forecasting literature graph models predict speeds or flows but are not connected to a signal timing law. Neither line of work uses class-aware counts from camera detections as node inputs, and neither addresses the limited field of view of a roadside camera.

## Vision-Based Traffic Sensing

Video processing for traffic began with background subtraction, edge analysis and blob tracking, methods that a survey found sensitive to illumination, shadows and occlusion [@22]. Single-stage convolutional detectors changed the cost of perception: YOLO predicts boxes and classes in one pass [@5], and the Ultralytics YOLOv8 release packages training and CPU or GPU inference for detectors of several sizes [@7]. A highway counting system built a set of 11,129 images with 57,290 annotated vehicles, trained YOLOv3 on its training split, tracked detections with ORB features and reached 93.2% counting accuracy [@3]. An improved YOLOv5 used Flip-Mosaic augmentation and a CIoU loss to detect small and occluded vehicles in highway surveillance [@6]. Other studies bypass detection. A convolutional network regressed traffic density directly from CCTV frames [@4], and deep networks classified camera images into congested and free-flow states [@23], [@24]. A hybrid Xception-SVM model classified Google Maps snapshots of Bengaluru into moderate and high congestion with 97.16% accuracy [@26]. Edge computing places detection close to the camera and reduces transmission load for congestion and speed monitoring [@30].

Indian traffic poses a data problem that generic benchmarks do not cover. The Urban Vision Hackathon dataset (UVH-26) addresses it with 26,646 images at 1080p from about 2,800 Bengaluru Safe-City cameras, 14 India-specific classes (bicycle, two-wheeler, three-wheeler, LCV, van, tempo-traveller, hatchback, sedan, SUV, MUV, mini-bus, bus, truck and other), and roughly 1.8 million crowdsourced boxes from 565 students, reconciled into consensus labels by majority voting and STAPLE [@8]. Detectors trained on UVH-26 improved mAP50:95 by 8.4% to 31.5% over COCO-trained counterparts, and the best model reached 0.67 against 0.40 for COCO weights on the classes common to both label sets [@8]. These results matter for control. A COCO-trained detector has no three-wheeler class, so an auto-rickshaw is either missed or scored as a car, motorcycle or truck, and the resulting lane score inherits that bias.

## Data-Driven Transportation Systems

Reviews of data-driven transportation systems describe a shift from model-driven to vision-driven, multisource-driven and learning-driven operation [@28]. A survey of deep learning for transportation lists data scarcity, interpretability and transfer across sites as the main obstacles to deployment [@29]. A review of deep learning for congestion concludes that most studies stop at detection or prediction and few close the loop to alleviation through control [@25]. The present study sits at that closing point: detections become class-weighted demand, demand becomes a graph prediction, and the prediction becomes green time through an explicit rule.

## Evidence Gaps and Positioning

Table 1 contrasts representative studies along six dimensions. Four gaps follow from it. First, no study in the set feeds class-aware camera detections of heterogeneous traffic into network-level coordination. Second, the camera field of view is ignored: a roadside camera observes roughly the first 60 m of an arm while queues on a 300 m link can exceed it, so a snapshot underestimates demand exactly when demand is highest, and no study tests how a policy trained on exact lane states behaves when it sees only that snapshot. Third, learned coordination sits inside black-box policies whose actions cannot be audited against a traffic engineering rule, which slows adoption by traffic police and city engineers. Fourth, evaluations often rely on one simulator run without a verified delay model, without classical baselines and without statistical tests. The present study targets all four gaps with a supervised graph attention estimator, a bounded timing law derived from Webster's model and SCATS-style tactical control, and a paired multi-seed evaluation on a simulator verified against Webster's delay formula.
